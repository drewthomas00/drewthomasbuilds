Who

How I got here

My dad was a software engineer and a consultant, so there was always a machine in pieces somewhere in the house. I caught it early and never put it down.

I wrote my first line of code in 2013, in Lua, inside Minecraft. I built a robot to do my mining for me so I could get back to building — and then spent the next year turning it into a fleet that quarried the land behind my house on its own. That was the whole thing right there: automate the boring part so you can go do the part you actually care about. I have been chasing that ever since.

I’ve worked since I was sixteen. Restaurants first — busboy, then barback, then expo, then serving by eighteen — and warehouses through college, including a long stretch on the night unload crew at one of the busiest FedEx Ground hubs in the country. None of it was software, and all of it mattered: how a real operation runs when it’s slammed and something goes wrong, how a small business actually makes money, how much a crew depends on you showing up on time, and what a dollar is worth once you’ve earned it that way.

What I actually wanted was to run something of my own. But I felt the financial pressure everyone feels at eighteen, and STEM looked like the safe answer. I set out aiming at biochemistry, then chemical engineering — until calculus and I reached an impasse. I took it twice and did worse the second time. So I went back to the thing I already knew I was good at, and studied Management Information Systems at the University of Alabama. I graduated in 2019 with a 3.8 overall and better than a 4.0 in my major, and I’ve been writing software professionally ever since — most of it on legacy systems for a mortgage lender, which is high-stakes, careful work and a very good teacher. The urge to build something of my own never went anywhere.

Then I did something most engineers don’t: I went to cosmetology school, as an actual career change. I meant it. I planned on a decade behind the chair, and I’m still there every Saturday — I’m good at it and I like it. What I didn’t plan on was that working a salon floor would show me a software problem I couldn’t put back down.

The company

Elovarra

Salon software is, almost without exception, built by people who have never worked a salon. You can feel it the moment you use it. It treats the day like a tidy grid of slots — not walk-ins, not color processing times, not a double-booked chair, not a client who will only ever sit for one stylist.

I hold the license and I’m still on the floor every Saturday, so I know where those tools break. They break on me. Elovarra is what I’m building instead.

Happy to go deep on any of that in a conversation.

Contract

Working together

I work full time and I’m building Elovarra, so I take on contract work sparingly and only when it’s a genuinely good fit. The engagements I’m best for usually look like one of these:

  • Own the build end to end

    You have an idea and no engineering team. I take it from nothing to something you can put in front of paying customers — which is exactly what I’ve spent the last six months doing for myself.

  • Modernize it without breaking it

    An old system with real revenue running through it, and no appetite for a rewrite that fails. I’ve kept a lender’s systems running under compliance pressure, and I know how to move carefully.

  • AI features that survive contact with users

    Not a chatbot bolted onto a landing page. Retrieval, evaluation, guardrails, and the boring parts that make it trustworthy.

    Salons, trades, local service businesses. I have unusual sympathy for this problem and a license to prove it.
