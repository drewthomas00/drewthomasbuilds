Quiet hours are not your hours

13 August 2026 · Compliance

Gate your marketing sends on the server clock and you are not compliant. You are compliant in one timezone, by accident.

The federal rule is short. 47 CFR 64.1200 bars telemarketing before 8 AM or after 9 PM — in the called party's local time. Four words that turn a trivial time check into a jurisdiction problem.

An 8:30 PM send from a California sender is lawful for a recipient in California and unlawful for one in Maryland. Same instant, same queue, same code path. The only thing that differs is where the phone is, which your scheduler does not know.

The window is not 8-to-9 everywhere

Several states are stricter than federal, and not in the same direction:

JurisdictionWindowSundays
Federal default8:00 AM – 9:00 PMpermitted
Maryland8:00 AM – 8:00 PMpermitted
Alabama, Florida, Louisiana, Mississippi8:00 AM – 8:00 PMbanned
Utah8:00 AM – 9:00 PMbanned
South Dakota9:00 AM – 9:00 PMbanned

So the check needs the recipient's state, not just their offset — and it needs their local weekday, because a Sunday blackout is evaluated on their calendar. Saturday 11:30 PM in California is already Sunday in Miami.

Area codes travel

The tempting shortcut is to derive a timezone from the phone number. It mostly works, and its failure mode is quiet.

Someone who moved from Chicago to Phoenix keeps their 312 number for a decade. You will place them in Central time, observing DST. They are in Arizona, which does not observe it. For half the year you are an hour off, in a system where being an hour off is the entire question.

A ZIP code is geographic and cannot follow a person, so it beats an area code whenever you have one. Both beat nothing. What matters is that the verdict records which signal it used, because six months later somebody will ask why a particular message went out at 8:30 PM and "we think it was the area code" is not an answer: 

{
  eligible: false,
  reason: 'outside_window',
  state: 'MD',
  timezone: 'America/New_York',
  source: 'zip',
  ruleApplied: 'MD'
}

Fail closed, including on the weekday

Every unresolvable input has to mean no. No timezone, an unparseable zone, a bad clock: all ineligible. An unknown recipient location is not permission.

The one that is easy to miss is the weekday. If you read the local day and cannot map it, the tempting default is "not Sunday" — and that quietly turns six state blackouts into a no-op. A day you could not read is not a day you may ignore.

There is a related trap in reading the clock at all. Some ICU builds report midnight as hour 24 rather than 0. Unhandled, a message at 00:15 evaluates as 24:15, every window comparison inverts, and the quiet-hours check starts permitting exactly the sends it exists to block.

Defer, do not drop

Knowing "not now" only lets you throw the message away. That is rarely what anyone wants — an operator who scheduled a 9:30 PM blast wants it at 8 AM, not deleted. So the useful question is not may I send this? but when may I?

Computing that is where a subtle bug lives. The obvious approach adds elapsed milliseconds until you land inside the window — but a DST transition in between moves the wall clock underneath the arithmetic. Aim at 8:00 AM across a spring-forward and you arrive at 9:00. Everything defers an hour late, twice a year, in a way that looks fine in every test written in June.

The fix is to re-read the recipient's clock after the jump and correct for the difference, rather than trusting that elapsed time and wall-clock time agree. They do not, and that is the whole reason this is hard.

Say what you have not verified

Encoding law in a lookup table creates an obligation the code cannot discharge. Statutes get amended; a table that was right when written goes stale silently, and its confidence never changes.

So each rule carries the statute it came from and a lastVerified date — and in the package this came out of, lastVerified is null on every rule, because they were compiled from secondary sources and I am not an attorney. That is a worse-looking field than a date. It is also the honest one, and it tells a reader exactly which claim to check before relying on it.

The evaluator is tcpa-quiet-hours — pure functions, no dependencies, and a test suite that runs under a non-UTC TZ, because a timezone library that only passes on a UTC machine has not been tested. Not legal advice.
